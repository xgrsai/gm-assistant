# Lviv in 1927: Setting Notes

Background for scenarios set in Lviv in the late 1920s. Historical details are simplified for play; everything tied to the supernatural is fiction.

## The City and Its People

### Political Situation

In 1927 Lviv (Polish: Lwów, German: Lemberg) is a large regional capital in the Second Polish Republic, which took control of the city after the Polish-Ukrainian war of 1918-1919. Official life, the administration, the police and the university are Polish. A large Ukrainian community and a large Jewish community live alongside the Polish majority, each with its own churches or synagogues, schools, societies and press. Tensions between the Polish authorities and Ukrainian organisations are a constant background of daily life.

### Everyday Life

The old centre around Rynok Square is crowded with trams, horse cabs and a growing number of motor cars. Gas lamps still light many side streets, and electric light is common only in wealthier buildings. Most people rent flats in multi-storey stone tenement houses (kamianytsia) built around courtyards, with a caretaker or landlady who knows everyone's business. Winters are damp and cold; November brings early darkness, rain and fog.

### Newspapers and Information

Ukrainian readers follow the daily newspaper "Dilo", founded in the nineteenth century; Polish and Yiddish papers serve the other communities. A journalist can gain access to police stations, hospitals, courts and archives with a press card, polite persistence and small favours. Public records, parish registers and university libraries are the usual places to research a person or a building.

## Places

### Pekarska Street and the Lychakiv District

Pekarska Street runs east from the city centre towards the hilly Lychakiv district. It is lined with tenement houses, small shops and bakeries, and it is known for the buildings of the university's medical faculty, where anatomy is taught. Students, doctors and lecturers rent rooms in the surrounding houses. Further up the hill lies the Lychakiv Cemetery, an old necropolis established in the late eighteenth century, crowded with sculpted tombs, crypts and overgrown paths.

### Beneath the Old City

Lviv stands on older foundations: filled-in cellars, forgotten crypts beneath churches and monasteries, old drainage channels and the covered bed of the Poltva river. Builders renovating cellars sometimes break into walled-up rooms or passages. Local folklore, which respectable people dismiss, speaks of burial vaults from the old plague years that were sealed and never mapped.

### The University and Its Anatomy Rooms

The medical faculty keeps a dissection hall, specimen collections preserved in formalin and a library of old medical texts. Bodies for dissection come from hospitals and from the unclaimed dead. Porters and night watchmen are poorly paid and can often be persuaded to open a door after hours.

## People an Investigator Might Meet

### Landladies and Caretakers

A tenement landlady collects rent, notices every visitor and keeps spare keys to the flats and the shared cellar. She fears scandal and the police in equal measure, and she will talk more freely to someone who is sympathetic and discreet.

### Police

The State Police (Policja Państwowa) handle missing persons. An officer is likely to treat a missing adult as someone who has left town voluntarily, especially without evidence of a crime, and is suspicious of Ukrainian journalists asking questions.

### Academics

University lecturers are respected figures, proud of their titles and careful about their reputations. Colleagues of a vanished professor are more willing to discuss his eccentric recent research than any hint of personal scandal.
